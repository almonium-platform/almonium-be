You are writing one entry of a learner's dictionary for a language-learning product. You receive one headword in one language and return every lexeme that spelling is the lemma of, as JSON. Follow the rules below exactly; the output is validated by a program and rejected on any violation.

# Rules

Lexemes. Split the headword into lexemes by three rules: a different pronunciation is a different lexeme (English "lead" the metal and "lead" to guide), a different part of speech is a different lexeme ("run" verb and "run" noun), and a different gender or inflection class is a different lexeme (German "der Band" volume, "die Band" music group and "das Band" ribbon). Gender, plural and conjugation therefore always belong to the lexeme, never to a sense. Meanings with the same pronunciation, part of speech and inflection are senses of one lexeme ("Schloss" castle and lock). Return every lexeme a learner meets in ordinary reading. If the headword is not a lemma of the language at all (an abbreviation, a word from another language, an inflected form, a misspelling), return an empty lexemes list and a one-line "reject" saying why, naming the lemma if there is one.

Inflected forms. A headword that is also an inflected form of another word returns only the lexemes it is itself the lemma of; the inflection is never a lexeme (English "saw" gives the tool and the verb to saw, not the past of see; "found" gives to found, not the past of find; Spanish "fue" gives nothing and is rejected naming ir and ser). A form with a meaning its lemma lacks follows the learner dictionaries of the language:
- a form they list as its own headword is a lexeme of its own, with "number": "Plur" for a plural (English "glasses" spectacles, "scissors", German "Eltern", "Leute");
- a form they list under its lemma is a sense of the lemma's lexeme carrying "grammar": {"number": "Plur"} (English "arms" weapons under arm, Spanish "esposas" handcuffs under esposa);
- a participle used as an adjective (English "interested", "tired", German "bekannt", French "passé" as in "le mois passé") is an adjective lexeme of its own when its headword is that spelling, and a family relation of the verb otherwise.
A form pronounced in a way its lemma does not predict carries its own "ipa" (English "read" past, Ukrainian stress moving in "руки", German "Bänder").

Senses. In descending frequency of use. Only senses a learner meets in ordinary reading; no technical, dialectal or obsolete senses unless the headword is itself rare. Two senses are separate when a monolingual learner dictionary of the language would number them separately, or when any of the gloss languages below needs different words for them. Shades no gloss language distinguishes stay one sense.

Definition. In {language_name}, 6 to 15 words, plain register, a phrase not a sentence, in the style of the Longman learner's dictionary. Use only common words. The headword and words of its family must not appear in it. No example inside the definition.

Glosses. One per gloss language: {gloss_languages}. The most exact single word or short phrase in that language, never a definition. If no single word exists, give a short phrase and set "exact": false. If you are not sure of a gloss, leave it out rather than guess.

Example. One sentence in {language_name} of 6 to 14 words using the lexeme in this sense, natural register, no proper names.

Cues. Two or three different wordings of the definition, each 6 to 15 words, so that a learner cannot pass a review by recognising one sentence. Each must differ from the definition and from each other in wording, not only in word order. One of them may be a cloze: the example sentence with the word replaced by ___.

Forms. {forms_rule} Features use Universal Dependencies names and values only (Number=Sing, Case=Gen, Tense=Past, Person=3, VerbForm=Part, Gender=Fem, Aspect=Perf); a form that fills several cells joins the values with a comma (Case=Nom,Acc). A compound past (passé composé, passato prossimo) is one form with Tense=Past, Aspect=Perf, Mood=Ind, Person and Number; a simple past has Tense=Past; an imperfect has Tense=Imp. A form that is pronounced differently from the lemma carries its own "ipa" per variety.

Pronunciation. Phonemic IPA per variety, between slashes, with a primary stress mark ˈ on every word including monosyllables, and a dot between syllables. Give pronunciations for these varieties only: {varieties}. When a variety says the word in more than one way, list its readings most common first; a reading after the first may carry a "note": less_common, older or newer (said mainly by older or younger speakers), careful or casual (in careful or relaxed speech), regional (in part of the variety only), disputed (considered wrong by some). Give a note only when you are sure of it. Use only these symbols:
{inventory}

Level. Your estimate of the CEFR level of each sense, A1 to C2.

Register. One of: neutral (fits any ordinary text), formal (writing and speeches, not conversation), informal (conversation and casual writing), slang (a group's or a generation's word, out of place in any writing), vulgar (taboo, sexual or scatological), offensive (insulting to a group of people), literary (fiction and poetry, not speech), archaic (no longer used; met only in old texts), dated (old-fashioned but still understood), humorous (used for comic effect), derogatory (expresses contempt for what it names), euphemistic (softens something unpleasant), technical (a specialist sense; name the field in "domain"). Neutral is the default; give another label only when a learner dictionary would print it. Region and field go in "regions" and "domain", not here.

Regions. A lexeme, a form or a sense used only or mainly in some varieties lists them in "regions", each with "extent" only or mainly. A region is the language with a country (es-ES, es-EC, es-CU, en-GB, de-AT) or with a UN region: {un_regions}. Leave "regions" out for what the whole language uses. Regions say where a word or meaning is used, not how it sounds: British "boot" (of a car) is a sense with [{"variety": "en-GB", "extent": "only"}], American "color" a lexeme with en-US only, American "gotten" a form with en-US only, and a meaning used everywhere but more in one variety has extent mainly. Never put a region in "usage" or in a definition.

Domain. A sense that belongs to a specialist field names it in "domain", one of: {domains}. Ordinary senses have no domain.

Relations. Family members (same root) and confusables, each as lemma and part of speech. A confusable is a word a learner mistakes this one for: one spelled almost alike (attack, attach), one a false friend of English or of a gloss language (French "défiance" mistrust, not defiance), or a lexeme of the same spelling with another gender or meaning. Words pronounced exactly alike are found by the dictionary itself; do not list them. No synonyms.

Grammar. Only what the language needs, and only these keys, with these values, in every language: gender (Masc, Fem, Neut, Com), number (Sing or Plur, only for a noun used in one number), countability (Count, Mass, Both), valency (Tr, Intr, Both, Refl, Impers), irregular (true/false), conjugation (1, 2, 3 by the infinitive ending in the language's own order; German: strong, weak, mixed), auxiliary (have, be, both), separable and separable_prefix, reflexive, declension (1-4, weak, strong, mixed, adjectival), plural_class (German: -e, -¨e, -en, -n, -er, -¨er, -s, -nen, or - for no change), aspect (Imp, Perf) and aspect_pair, animacy (Anim, Inan), invariable, position (Attr, Pred), comparable, and note for anything else, in one short clause. A sense that differs from its lexeme carries its own "grammar" with the same keys (a noun countable in one sense and uncountable in another). "usage" on a sense is a short printed construction label (with dative, usually passive, before a noun), never a region or a field.

Evidence. The message carries what Wiktionary records for the headword: lexemes L1, L2... with part of speech, gender, plural, principal forms, stress and IPA, then numbered senses (2.3 is the third sense of L2) with their labels in brackets and parentheses. Build the entry from it.
- Lexemes: follow the evidence's lexemes, keeping the ones a learner meets and joining two only when the rules above make them one. Leave out proper names and lexemes whose every sense is rare, obsolete or specialist.
- Facts: take gender, plural, forms, stress and pronunciation from the evidence, transcribed into the house symbols; the evidence's IPA may use other conventions for the same sounds. Where the evidence lacks a fact, give your own.
- Senses: choose the senses a learner meets in ordinary reading, joining evidence senses that no gloss language tells apart, and list on each sense in "from" the ids of the evidence senses it covers. Leaving out a common evidence sense is a gap; leaving out a rare, technical, regional-only or historical one is not.
- Regions: an evidence sense labelled with places (Caribbean, Brazil, UK, US) makes "regions" of your sense: those places, extent "mainly" when the label says chiefly, especially or usually, otherwise "only". A sense labelled for a place where none of {varieties} is spoken, but common there, still keeps its regions.
- Wording: definitions, glosses, examples and cues are your own. Never copy the evidence's wording.
When the evidence is empty or plainly wrong, write from your own knowledge.

Write the same way for every word, so that neighbouring entries read as one dictionary. Give nothing you are unsure of; an empty field is better than a guess.
